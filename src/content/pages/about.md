I am Nick. Ember is the dog. We walk, mostly in the Peak District and around
Charnwood, and this is where the routes end up.

Nothing here is a guidebook route retyped. Every walk on this site is one we
actually did, on a day with real weather, recorded on the move. The line on the
map is the line we walked, including the bits where we got it wrong.

## Why another walking site

Because of one phrase: **dog friendly**.

Nearly every walking site in the country will tell you a route is dog friendly
and then stop, as though that settles it. It settles nothing. It does not tell
you there are eleven stiles and your labrador cannot climb. It does not tell you
the last mile is a verge on a B road. It does not tell you there is no water for
four miles in August, or that the field at the top has ewes in it every spring.

Those are the things that decide whether a walk is a good day or a long
apology to your dog. So they are written down here, on every route, as a set of
icons you can read in about three seconds: stiles, livestock, water, roads,
ground underfoot, whether there is anywhere sensible to let a dog run, and
whether you are on Access land where the lead is a legal requirement from
1 March to 31 July rather than a courtesy.

If that sounds obsessive, it is. It is also the entire reason this site exists.

## What you get for each walk

- The **GPX from the day**, so you have the actual line rather than an
  approximation
- Distance, ascent and how long it genuinely took, from the recording and not
  from an estimate
- The **grid reference and postcode of where the car went**, because a sat nav
  wants one and a paper map wants the other
- What the weather was doing, in degrees and miles per hour
- Photographs from the walk itself, in the order we came to them
- The dog detail above, on every single route

## What you will not get

Turn by turn directions. No numbered steps, no "bear left at the third gate".

Partly because I would rather be walking than writing them, and mostly because
that is not the point. Directions that granular go stale the moment a farmer
moves a fence, and following someone else's numbered list is not navigating, it
is just obedience with a view. The point is to show you a route that works with
a dog, give you the line, and let you find the ground yourself.

Take the GPX. Take a map. Both, ideally.

## An honest word about the gradings

Easy, moderate and hard are my opinions, formed by my legs on the day I walked
it. A moderate in dry June is not a moderate in wet February, and a route that
was fine for Ember may not suit a dog with shorter legs, a heavier coat or an
older set of hips.

Everything here is planning support, not a substitute for checking the forecast,
carrying a map and turning round when the day says turn round. Conditions
change. Access changes. Livestock appears where there was none last time. Use
your judgement, because it is your dog and your day.

## Getting in touch

If a route has changed, a path is closed, a car park has started charging or I
have simply got something wrong, I would genuinely like to know. Corrections
from people who have actually been there are worth more than anything I can
write from memory.
